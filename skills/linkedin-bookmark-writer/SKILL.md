# LinkedIn Bookmark Writer

Write for bookmarks, not likes. A like costs the reader nothing. A save means the post gave them something they want to reuse: a number to benchmark against, a framework, a decision rule, a mistake they no longer have to make. Every post should be something the target reader wants to save, send to their team, or come back to.

## Before you write

1. **Look for a voice profile.** If the writer has one (in the conversation, in memory, in a project file, or a filled-in copy of `references/voice-profile.md`), use it. It defines the target reader, what the writer sells or stands for, names to protect, cleared references, house naming, and personal bans. If there is none, do not stall: write with the defaults in this file, and after the post offer once to set one up from the template.
2. **Identify the reader.** Default: the senior person in the writer's field who decides or influences decisions (a buyer, a peer leader, an investor, a hiring manager). Infer from the material when the writer does not say.
3. **Run the bookmark test** on the raw material (below).
4. **Verify claims** (see Accuracy and reputation).

## The bookmark test

A post is bookmark-worthy when a reader would come back to it for at least one of:

- a specific number or timeframe they can benchmark against
- a mistake the writer made, and what it cost
- a reusable decision rule, checklist, or framework
- a contrast between two approaches with a clear tradeoff

If the material has none of these, flag it plainly ("As written, this is a like post, not a bookmark post") and suggest the one addition that would fix it, phrased as a question the writer can answer:

- "What did this cost you, in weeks or dollars?"
- "What rule would you give a peer facing the same choice?"
- "What did you get wrong the first time?"

Still deliver a best-effort draft unless the writer asked only for critique.

## Pick a style

Use the style the writer asks for (if they say "generic", they mean Framework). If they do not say, choose: lessons, mistakes, observations, and personal stories go to **Thought leadership**; announcements, explainers, and frameworks tied to what the writer offers go to **Framework**. If it is truly ambiguous, write Framework and mention the other style is available. Do not ask before drafting; a draft is cheap and gives the writer something to react to.

### Framework style

- A sharp hook.
- A layered insight or framework, where each layer goes one level deeper than the last.
- The writer's product, service, or point of view positioned as the natural answer. The reader should arrive at it, not be told. Name it once, late in the post.
- A soft, understated call to action: an invitation, never a demand.
- Short paragraphs, plain prose.

### Thought leadership style

- Bite-sized points.
- Informal and slightly messy tone. It should sound like a person thinking, not a brochure.
- Ideas drawn from things learned the hard way.
- No pitch and no product mention.
- A deliberately unfinished ending instead of a tidy conclusion: the question the writer is still working on, or a tension left open. This is different from engagement bait. An unfinished ending says what the writer is still unsure about; bait asks the reader to do work for the algorithm.

## Hooks and structure

- **The opening line must stop the scroll.** Use a bold specific claim, a counterintuitive observation, a surprising number, or a real problem named directly. No warm-up, no scene-setting, no "I've been thinking about".
- **The first two lines must work on their own,** since that is often all a reader sees before the post is truncated.
- **Every line earns the next.** The post should pull harder at paragraph three than at paragraph one. If a paragraph could be deleted without loss, delete it.
- **One central idea per post.** If the input has several, pick the strongest and use the rest as support. List the leftovers after the post as candidates for future posts.
- **Self-contained.** The post delivers its full value without a link or a comment. If a link matters, it goes in the first comment and the post still stands alone.

## Specificity

Include at least two of:

- a real number or timeframe
- a named stakeholder or persona (the CFO, the first sales hire, a new manager)
- a concrete example from the writer's experience
- a clear tradeoff
- a contrast between two approaches
- a practical implication: what the reader should do differently on Monday

Never invent numbers, quotes, customers, or experiences to meet this bar. Use only what the writer gave you. Where a specific is missing, leave a visible placeholder such as `[number: how many weeks did this take?]` and list the placeholders after the post.

## Four hooks with every post

After every post, give four alternative opening lines, exactly one from each category:

1. **Contrarian:** disagrees with a belief most people in the field hold.
2. **Counterintuitive:** a true statement that sounds wrong at first.
3. **Authority:** leads with a credential, scale, or result that earns the right to speak.
4. **Experience:** opens inside a specific moment the writer lived through.

Authority and Experience hooks may only use numbers and experiences the writer has actually shared. If they have not shared any, write the hook with a placeholder and say what is needed.

## Absolute bans

These apply to the post and to the hooks.

- **No em dashes or en dashes, anywhere.** Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead. Hyphens inside compound words are fine.
- **Never use "honest" or "honestly".** The words imply the rest of the post is not.
- **No markdown in the post itself:** no bold, italics, headers, or asterisks, since LinkedIn does not render them. Use • for bullets when a list is needed. Line breaks are fine.
- **No preamble.** Never open with "Here's your LinkedIn post". Deliver the post directly.
- **No hashtags** unless the writer asks for them. If the writer asks for an X version, no hashtags there at all.
- **No emojis** unless the source material uses them.
- **No banned phrases:** "in today's world", "game changer", "the future belongs to", "let that sink in", "read that again", "we are still early", "unlocking potential", "at the end of the day". Avoid their close cousins too ("game-changing", "in today's fast-paced world").
- **No weak engagement bait endings:** "Thoughts?", "Agree?", "What do you think?", "Comment below", "Like if you agree".
- **At most one extended metaphor per post,** whatever the writer's signature one is (sailing, sports, cooking). Stacked metaphors read as filler.

The voice profile can add personal bans on top of these. It cannot remove these.

## Accuracy and reputation

- **Verify** any news, statistic, quote, or third-party claim before writing, using search when available. If something is wrong, outdated, or unverifiable, flag the correction up front in one or two lines, then write the post on the corrected facts.
- **Paraphrase** articles rather than quoting long passages, especially paywalled ones, and credit the source by name.
- **No competitor trash talk.** Never name a company in a negative context ("X got this wrong", "unlike X"). Make the point about the approach, not the company. Take extra care with the partners, customers, employers, platforms, and investors listed in the voice profile.
- **Cleared references only.** Confirm customer, client, employer, and government references are cleared for public use before naming them. If not confirmed, use a descriptor ("a Fortune 100 retailer", "a state agency") and flag it.
- **Analysts and research firms:** many restrict how their names, reports, and placements are used publicly. Do not cite them unless the writer confirms the use is cleared.

## House naming

If the voice profile lists product, team, or program names, use them exactly as written, including capitalization, spacing, and the current name rather than a former one. If the writer separates their category (what they are) from their attributes (how they differ), keep the category in the category slot and the attributes as attributes. Without a profile, keep the writer's own spelling of every name in the source material.

## Output format

In this order:

1. **Flags**, only if there are any: corrections from verification, uncleared references, or a failed bookmark test. One line each.
2. **The post**, as plain text, ready to paste.
3. **Four hooks**, labeled Contrarian, Counterintuitive, Authority, Experience.
4. **Placeholders to fill** and leftover ideas for future posts, only if there are any.
5. **Bookmark check:** one line naming what makes the post worth saving, or the one thing that would.

## Final check before delivering

If you can run code, save the post to a text file and run the bundled linter from this skill's folder: `python scripts/lint_post.py post.txt`. It catches dashes, banned words and phrases, markdown, hashtags, emojis, and bait endings. Fix everything it reports. Then check by eye:

- Does the first line stop the scroll on its own?
- Is there exactly one central idea?
- Are there at least two specificity elements, none invented?
- Does it end with a soft call to action (Framework) or a deliberately open thought (Thought leadership), never bait?
- Is any company named negatively? Are all named references cleared?
- Are there exactly four hooks, one per category, with Authority and Experience built only on shared facts?

## Rewriting and critiquing existing drafts

- **Rewrite:** keep the writer's voice, facts, and names. Fix the structure and the bans. Do not add claims.
- **Critique only:** lead with the bookmark verdict, then the top three fixes in order of impact, then a rewritten opening line.

See `references/examples.md` for one worked example in each style, from raw notes to finished post and hooks.

# Worked examples

Both examples use invented writers. Every number and experience in each post comes from that writer's raw notes, which is the rule: nothing gets added.

## Example 1: Framework style

**Raw notes from the writer** (founder of a customer onboarding software company):
"Looked back at 40 customer onboardings from last year. The ones with a named executive sponsor went live in about 6 weeks, without one about 14. Same product, same implementation team, similar scope. The stall is always the first decision that crosses two departments. On one project, at week five, I asked who could break the tie between IT and operations. Nobody could answer. We now build a sponsor checkpoint into our onboarding flow. Want people planning rollouts to see this."

**Post:**

Onboarding projects don't stall on software. They stall on the week nobody owns.

We looked back at 40 customer onboardings from last year. The ones with a named executive sponsor went live in about 6 weeks. The ones without took about 14.

Same product. Same implementation team. Similar scope.

The gap opened in one place: the first decision that crossed two departments.

• With a sponsor, it got made in a day.
• Without one, it sat in a thread until someone escalated.

So the rule we now give every customer is simple. Before kickoff, write down the one person who can break a tie between IT and the business. If you can't name them, you're not ready to start.

We built that checkpoint into our onboarding flow after seeing the same 8 week gap again and again. If you're planning a rollout next quarter, the sponsor question is the cheapest fix on the list.

**Hooks:**
- Contrarian: Most onboarding advice is about better software. The fix we found had nothing to do with software.
- Counterintuitive: Adding one person to a project cut its timeline by more than half.
- Authority: We tracked 40 onboardings last year. One question at kickoff split them into 6 week and 14 week projects.
- Experience: Week five of a stalled rollout, I asked who could break the tie between IT and operations. Nobody could answer.

**Bookmark check:** a benchmark (6 vs 14 weeks across 40 projects) plus a decision rule a reader can apply before their next kickoff.

## Example 2: Thought leadership style

**Raw notes from the writer** (first-time manager, now a director):
"My first three months managing, I rewrote my team's work at night after they logged off. Thought I was protecting quality. Two people quit. What I do now: same-day feedback in the doc, never rewrite quietly, say out loud what good enough looks like. Still don't know what to do when a client deliverable is due tomorrow and it's not there."

**Post:**

For three months as a new manager I rewrote my team's work at night.

I thought I was protecting quality. I was teaching them their drafts didn't matter.

Two people left before I figured it out.

What I do differently now:

• Feedback the same day, in the doc, while it's still their work
• I never quietly rewrite. If it needs a rewrite, we do it together, once
• "Good enough to ship" gets said out loud, so nobody guesses where the bar is

The part I still haven't solved: the client deliverable that's due tomorrow and isn't there yet. Rewriting it is faster. Coaching it is better. I don't know where that line sits.

**Hooks:**
- Contrarian: Fixing your team's work is the fastest way to lower its quality.
- Counterintuitive: The nights I spent improving my team's work made it worse.
- Authority: I've managed teams for [number: how many years?]. My most expensive habit showed up in the first three months.
- Experience: Most nights for three months, I'd open my team's drafts after they logged off and start rewriting.

**Placeholders to fill:** years of management experience, for the Authority hook.

**Bookmark check:** a mistake with a real cost (two resignations) and three rules a new manager can adopt this week.
